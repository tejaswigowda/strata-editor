Hardy–Weinberg as a square

A 75-second visual proof of the Hardy–Weinberg law, p² + 2pq + q² = 1, which is nothing but (p + q)² = 1 with the square labelled. A gene has two alleles, A with frequency p and a with frequency q, and every child gets one from each parent at random: draw a square, cut each side into a part p and a part q, and each of the four pieces is one kind of child, with an area equal to how common that kind is. The two off-diagonal pieces are the same kind (Aa), which is why the middle term is 2pq and not pq. It is the same visual logic as the three slabs of 3a²b in the (a + b)³ tutorial, one dimension down, and it reuses that tutorial's recipe (a room, unit tiles, shadows, a row of coloured labels, a camera sequence) rather than starting from nothing. Open the editor, load an empty scene, open the Shell tab and paste each block in order, or open the finished scene, hw.json (the last step).

1 The idea: a square of children

Take one gene with two alleles. A fraction p of the gene copies in the population are A (the dominant one) and a fraction q are a, and since there are no others, p + q = 1. With random mating each parent hands on one of its two copies at random, independently, so the chance that a child gets A from the first parent and a from the second is p × q, a product of two frequencies, which is exactly what an area is. A unit square, with its top edge cut into p and q (the first parent) and its left edge cut into p and q (the second), is therefore a table of every possible child:

ColourPieceChildSidesArea
coraltop leftAAp × pp²
goldtop rightAaq × ppq
goldbottom leftaAp × qpq
aquabottom rightaaq × qq²

Both gold pieces are heterozygotes: Aa and aA are the same child, whichever parent gave which allele. That is why they share a colour, and why the film puts them side by side under the middle term of the equation, as two rectangles. The total is the whole square: p² + pq + pq + q² = (p + q)² = 1. The law itself is the observation that this is also the next generation's table: with random mating the proportions of AA, Aa and aa stay p², 2pq and q², generation after generation, unless something (selection, drift, migration, non-random mating) changes them.

The drawing uses p = 3 tiles and q = 2 tiles so the two parts look different; the film never says a number. The plan for the clock (all times in seconds):

3 The square: 25 tiles in four groups

The square is built from unit tiles, as in the Pythagorean short, one shared geometry and one shared material per colour, and each piece is a group of its tiles placed relative to the middle of the piece. A group is what lets a piece fly, shrink and turn as a body, and it is how the editor sees "the pink piece" as one thing. The table in PIECES says, for each piece, its width and height in tiles and the middle of the piece in the whole square. The top-left piece is p wide and p tall (g-pp), the bottom-right q by q (g-qq), and the two off-diagonal ones are q wide and p tall (g-pq1) and p wide and q tall (g-pq2): the same rectangle, once upright and once lying down.

// the square: p = 3 tiles and q = 2 tiles on a side (the drawing is 3 and 2 so the two parts look different; the film says p and q).
// It is the Pythagorean short's squares of unit tiles, cut into four pieces: rows and columns split at p.
const P = 3, Q = 2, H = (P + Q) / 2;
const COLOR = { pp: 0xff4d6a, pq: 0xffb703, qq: 0x14e0c8 };           // AA coral, Aa gold, aa aqua
const unit = new BoxGeometry(0.94, 0.94, 0.94);                         // one shared cube, a small gap between tiles
const mat = Object.fromEntries(Object.entries(COLOR).map(([k, c]) =>
  [k, new MeshStandardMaterial({ color: c, emissive: c, emissiveIntensity: 0.25, roughness: 0.5, metalness: 0.1 })]));

// name, kind, width and height in tiles, and the middle of the piece in the whole square
const PIECES = [
  ['g-pp',  'pp', P, P, -H + P / 2,     H - P / 2],       // top left: p wide, p tall          AA   p^2
  ['g-pq1', 'pq', Q, P,  H - Q / 2,     H - P / 2],       // top right: q wide, p tall           Aa   pq
  ['g-pq2', 'pq', P, Q, -H + P / 2,    -H + Q / 2],       // bottom left: p wide, q tall         aA   pq
  ['g-qq',  'qq', Q, Q,  H - Q / 2,    -H + Q / 2]        // bottom right: q wide, q tall        aa   q^2
];
for (const [name, kind, w, h, cx, cy] of PIECES) {
  const group = new Group();
  group.name = name;
  group.position.set(cx, cy, 0);
  for (let i = 0; i < w; i++) for (let j = 0; j < h; j++) {
    const tile = new Mesh(unit, mat[kind]);
    tile.name = `${name}.${i}.${j}`;
    tile.position.set(i + 0.5 - w / 2, j + 0.5 - h / 2, 0);              // relative to the middle of the piece
    tile.castShadow = tile.receiveShadow = true;
    tile.userData.hasEdgeOutline = true;                                  // a black border, drawn by the viewport
    group.add(tile);
  }
  group.userData.home = [cx, cy];
  editor.execute(new AddObjectCommand(editor, group));
}
$S('#g-pp').addClass('pp');
$S('#g-qq').addClass('qq');
$S('#g-pq1, #g-pq2').addClass('pq');
$S('.pp, .pq, .qq').addClass('piece');
// a class on a group also matches everything inside it (each tile's border too), so count the tiles by name
$S('.piece').nodes.filter(n => n.isMesh && n.name.startsWith('g-')).length      // 25 tiles: 9 + 6 + 6 + 4
A class on a group matches the group's contents too. $S('.piece') returns the four groups and their tiles, and each tile's black border besides, so the count at the end of the block filters by name. This is the same thing the Pythagorean short's note about .cube says; here it matters whenever a verb is applied to a class: .fade on .piece would fade the tiles one by one, which is what you want for a glass effect but not for a move.

5 Four cameras

A shot camera is an ordinary camera with a track of keys: setKeys([{ t, pos, lookAt }, …], { smooth: true }) plays one smooth curve through them (see Editing camera moves). orbit writes the keys from an azimuth, a radius and a height. Because this scene is flat, the cameras stay nearly head-on (azimuth within ±8°): a square seen from the side is a sliver, and the equation above it, a row of text that faces the front, would shrink. Each is aimed a little above the middle of what it films. The second one is further back and aimed lower, because during the sort the pieces fly up towards the equation, and the third pulls back for the finished square.

// four cameras: a slow push-in on the whole square, a closer one for the sorting, a wide one for the reassembly, and one for the end card
function addCamera(name, fov) {
  const cam = new THREE.PerspectiveCamera(fov, 16 / 9, 0.1, 400);
  cam.name = name;
  editor.execute(new AddObjectCommand(editor, cam));
  return cam;
}

// a move around a point: azimuth in degrees (0 = straight in front), a radius and a height, one key every `step` seconds
function orbit(cam, t0, t1, az0, az1, radius, y0, y1, aim, step = 4) {
  const n = Math.max(1, Math.round((t1 - t0) / step));
  const keys = [];
  for (let i = 0; i <= n; i++) {
    const u = i / n, az = THREE.MathUtils.degToRad(az0 + (az1 - az0) * u);
    keys.push({ t: t0 + (t1 - t0) * u, pos: [radius * Math.sin(az), y0 + (y1 - y0) * u, radius * Math.cos(az)], lookAt: aim });
  }
  $S('#' + cam.name).setKeys(keys, { smooth: true });
  cam.position.set(...keys[0].pos);           // where the camera waits before its move starts
  cam.lookAt(...aim);
}

const camA = addCamera('camA', 40), camB = addCamera('camB', 40), camC = addCamera('camC', 40), end = addCamera('camCard', 50);
orbit(camA, 0.001, 29.5, -5, 6, 16.5, 3.5, 3, [0, 1.6, 0]);        // a little push round, nearly head-on
orbit(camB, 29.5, 51.5, 6, -6, 18, 2.5, 2, [0.3, 0.9, 0]);          // further back and aimed lower: the pieces fly apart, then up to the row
orbit(camC, 51.5, 72.8, -6, 6, 16.5, 3, 4, [0, 1.6, 0]);            // pulls back for the whole square
end.position.set(14.6, 0.5, 0);                                      // faces the end card, which hangs on the right-hand wall
end.lookAt(30, 0.5, 0);

6 The labels

Open Stencils → Mesh → Text and click it once; the next block copies that one label for every other, so they all share its font. A label's text starts at the label's own origin, so to centre a label you subtract half its width. The widths come from the font: the sum of its glyphs' advances, in units of the font size, times the label's scale.

TextWidthTextWidth
(p + q)²4.772pq2.49
=   +   10.83 eachq²1.39

At a scale of 0.6 and a gap of 0.45 between nine labels the equation is 12.1 wide, so its first label starts at −6.05, and each next one at the sum of the widths and gaps before it. The letters on the pieces are children of their piece (parent.add(label)), so they travel, shrink and turn with it.

// every label is a copy of the one Text stencil, so they share its font. A label's text begins at its own origin, so to
// centre one you subtract half its width: the widths come from the font, in units of the font size (see the table below).
const tpl = $S('#Text').nodes[0];
const WHITE = 0xffffff, CORAL = 0xff4d6a, GOLD = 0xffb703, AQUA = 0x14e0c8;
let first = true;
function label(name, color, x, y, z, scale, parent) {
  const m = first ? tpl : new Mesh(tpl.geometry.clone(), tpl.material.clone());
  m.name = name;
  m.material.color = new Color(color);
  m.material.emissive = new Color(color);
  m.material.emissiveIntensity = 0.7;
  m.scale.setScalar(scale);
  m.position.set(x, y, z);
  m.userData.textShadow = true;
  m.castShadow = false;
  if (!first) editor.execute(new AddObjectCommand(editor, m));
  first = false;
  if (parent) parent.add(m);                         // hang it on a piece, so it travels with it
  return m;
}

// the equation, nine labels in a row, at scale 0.6 with 0.45 between them: (p + q)^2 = p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1
const EQ = [['lead', WHITE, -6.05], ['eq1', WHITE, -2.74], ['tP', CORAL, -1.79], ['pl1', WHITE, -0.51], ['tPQ', GOLD, 0.43],

Ids are matched without regard to case. A label called kAA and another called kaa are the same id to the selector engine, so one of them is silently left out of any set that names both. The first version of this scene did exactly that, and it showed aa on the coral piece. Name labels for where they are (kidPP, kidQQ), never for what they say.

10 The square falls apart, and the pieces sort themselves

At 29.8 s each piece steps a little away from the middle (APART), so that the four are separate and can be named. The voice then names each one and the labels come in: AA, Aa, Aa, aa. Then, as each term of the equation appears, its piece flies to a spot under it and shrinks to 42% (scaleX/Y/Z are absolute). The two gold pieces are the interesting part: they fly under 2pq together, one above the other, so that the middle term looks like what it is, two pieces. The tall one turns a quarter turn (rotateZ: 90) to lie like the other, and its label turns the other way (-90) so that the letters stay upright. At 51.9 s everything flies back, the turned piece turns back, and the square is whole.

// the pieces step apart, then each kind flies to its place under its own term of the equation, smaller (scale 0.42),
// and the two Aa rectangles are stacked: the one that is q wide and p tall turns a quarter turn to lie like the other.
// Positions are in the world, the pieces' groups sit at their middles.
const S = 0.42;

13 Render it

  1. Open the Render tab. The Camera Sequence already holds the four shots from step 8.
  2. Pick 1280 × 720 and 30 fps, and click Render & Export. It runs in real time, so a little over a minute. Tick Hard-burn to draw the subtitles into the picture, and keep Also export a matching .srt.
  3. For captions a viewer can switch off, render without the burn and add the .srt as a soft track afterwards: ffmpeg -i film.mp4 -i film.srt -c copy -c:s mov_text -metadata:s:s:0 language=eng film-subs.mp4.
Everything above is git-versioned. Commit from the Git tab whenever you like: the geometry, the timeline, the camera sequence and the subtitles all live in the scene file.

14 Open the finished scene

hw.json in tejaswigowda/test1 is the scene these blocks build: 4 groups of 25 tiles, the outline, 4 rulers and 4 ruler letters, 2 cuts, 9 equation labels, 4 piece labels, 4 cameras and the end card, with 35 timeline tracks, 4 shots and 13 subtitle cues. Open it, then scrub the Animations tab: at 3 s the square is whole, at 31 s it is in four pieces, at 50 s each kind is under its term, at 57 s it is whole again, and from 73 s the card fills the frame.

What was checked, and what differs. Every block on this page was pasted, one paste at a time, in this order, into an empty scene, with the Text stencil and the Call card stencil clicked where the text says so; the scene was then committed, loaded again from the commit, and played at three times (3, 33 and 57 s). The screenshots on this page are frames of the pasted scene at 2, 20, 27, 31, 38, 50, 57 and 76 s, with the editor's controls hidden. The cues were run through a speech synthesizer and fit. Rendering to a video file (step 13) was not run for this page. This is not the Pythagorean short's pyth.json relabelled: it is a new scene with the same ingredients (a square of unit tiles, the room, the labels, the camera sequence), because the pieces here are cut at p and q, not at 3-4-5.
