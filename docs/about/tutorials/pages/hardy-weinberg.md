# Hardy–Weinberg as a square

<div class="wc-embed" title="Hardy–Weinberg as a square: interactive example"><a href="https://strataeditor.dev/#repo=tejaswigowda/test1@89f9272c7b891ab7bf798a2b58de4b3a08a36eab&amp;file=hw.json&amp;present=true">Open embed</a></div>

<p class="tagline">A 115-second film about the Hardy–Weinberg law. A square shows the <em>genotype proportions that one round of random mating produces</em>: <em>(p + q)² = p² + 2pq + q²</em>, with <em>p² + 2pq + q² = 1</em> because the whole square has area one. A gene has two alleles, <em>A</em> with frequency <em>p</em> and <em>a</em> with frequency <em>q</em>, and every child gets one from each parent at random: draw a square, cut each side into a part <em>p</em> and a part <em>q</em>, and each of the four pieces is one kind of child, with an area equal to how common that kind is. The two off-diagonal pieces are the same kind (<em>Aa</em>), which is why the middle term is <em>2pq</em> and not <em>pq</em>. That much is the expansion of a square, true with no biology in it. A second beat, a count of the alleles in the children, <em>shows that the allele frequencies are unchanged by that mating</em>, and the film then says under which assumptions the same proportions come back in every generation. The square is the same visual logic as the three slabs of <em>3a²b</em> in the <a href="https://strataeditor.dev/about/tutorials/a-plus-b-cubed.html">(a + b)³ tutorial</a>, one dimension down, and it reuses that tutorial&#39;s recipe (a room, unit tiles, shadows, a row of coloured labels, a camera sequence). Open the <a href="https://strataeditor.dev/">editor</a>, load an empty scene, open the <strong>Shell</strong> tab and paste each block in order, or open the finished scene, <a href="https://strataeditor.dev/#repo=tejaswigowda/test1&amp;file=hw.json&amp;branch=main">hw.json</a> (<a href="#open-finished">the last step</a>).</p>

<h2><span class="step">1</span> The idea: a square of children</h2>

<p class="lede">Take one gene with two alleles. A fraction <em>p</em> of the gene copies in the population are <em>A</em> (the dominant one) and a fraction <em>q</em> are <em>a</em>, and since there are no others, <em>p + q = 1</em>. With random mating each parent hands on one of its two copies at random, independently, so the chance that a child gets <em>A</em> from the first parent and <em>a</em> from the second is <em>p × q</em>, a product of two frequencies, which is exactly what an area is. A unit square, with its top edge cut into <em>p</em> and <em>q</em> (the first parent) and its left edge cut into <em>p</em> and <em>q</em> (the second), is therefore a table of every possible child:</p>

<table class="cuts">&#10;		<tr><th>Colour</th><th>Piece</th><th>Child</th><th>Sides</th><th>Area</th></tr>&#10;		<tr><td class="legacy-style-1">coral</td><td>top left</td><td>AA</td><td>p × p</td><td>p²</td></tr>&#10;		<tr><td class="legacy-style-2">gold</td><td>top right</td><td>Aa</td><td>q × p</td><td>pq</td></tr>&#10;		<tr><td class="legacy-style-3">gold</td><td>bottom left</td><td>aA</td><td>p × q</td><td>pq</td></tr>&#10;		<tr><td class="legacy-style-4">aqua</td><td>bottom right</td><td>aa</td><td>q × q</td><td>q²</td></tr>&#10;	</table>

Both gold pieces are heterozygotes: _Aa_ and _aA_ are the same child, whichever parent gave which allele. That is why they share a colour, and why the film puts them _side by side_ under the middle term of the equation, as **two** rectangles. The total is the whole square: _p² + pq + pq + q² = (p + q)² = 1_.

<p><strong>What the square shows, and what it does not.</strong> The square gives the proportions of the three kinds of child in <em>one</em> round of random mating, whatever <em>p</em> and <em>q</em> are. It does not, by itself, say that those proportions come back next time: for that the allele frequencies in the children must be the same <em>p</em> and <em>q</em> as in the parents. So the film has a second beat. It takes the children apart into their alleles (<em>AA</em> has two <em>A</em>, <em>Aa</em> has one of each, <em>aa</em> has two <em>a</em>), sorts all of them into two bins, and measures the bins with the same two rulers that measured the parents. Here, with <em>p</em> = 0.6 and <em>q</em> = 0.4: the 9 <em>AA</em>, 12 <em>Aa</em> and 4 <em>aa</em> children of a group of 25 hold 18 + 12 = 30 <em>A</em> and 12 + 8 = 20 <em>a</em>, 50 alleles in all, so <em>p′</em> = 30/50 = 0.6 and <em>q′</em> = 20/50 = 0.4, exactly the parents&#39; values. <strong>The allele frequencies <em>p</em> and <em>q</em> are unchanged from one generation to the next: that is the result, and the genotype proportions follow from it.</strong></p>

That holds only under assumptions, and the film lists them on screen while the voice says them: _random mating_, _no selection_, _no mutation_, _no migration_, and _a large (effectively infinite) population_, because in a small one chance alone moves the frequencies (drift). If any of the five fails, _p_ and _q_ can change, and the proportions with them.

The drawing uses _p_ = 3 tiles and _q_ = 2 tiles so the two parts look different. The only numbers the film uses are the counts of the allele beat, spoken as words (fifty, thirty, twenty), and the 1 in the equation; the 0.6 and 0.4 above are this drawing's frequencies, for the arithmetic only. The plan for the clock (all times in seconds):

<h2><span class="step">2</span> A room, a wall and a floor</h2>

<p class="lede">The same room as in the cube tutorial, with one addition: a <em>wall</em> just behind the square, so that the square has something close to throw its shadow on. A flat figure in a big empty room throws its shadow far away and soft; a wall 1.1 units behind it gives a tight, readable shadow. The wall is only 26 wide: the end camera sits at x = 14.6 and looks along +x, and a wider wall would be seen edge-on in its frame.</p>

<h2><span class="step">3</span> The square: 25 tiles in four groups</h2>

<p class="lede">The square is built from unit tiles, as in the Pythagorean short, one shared geometry and one shared material per colour (the next steps reuse those three materials for the rulers, the tokens and the coloured labels, so the whole film has <strong>nine materials</strong>), and each piece is a <em>group</em> of its tiles placed relative to the middle of the piece. A group is what lets a piece fly, shrink and turn as a body, and it is how the editor sees &quot;the pink piece&quot; as one thing. The table in <code>PIECES</code> says, for each piece, its width and height in tiles and the middle of the piece in the whole square. The top-left piece is <em>p</em> wide and <em>p</em> tall (<code>g-pp</code>), the bottom-right <em>q</em> by <em>q</em> (<code>g-qq</code>), and the two off-diagonal ones are <em>q</em> wide and <em>p</em> tall (<code>g-pq1</code>) and <em>p</em> wide and <em>q</em> tall (<code>g-pq2</code>): the same rectangle, once upright and once lying down.</p>

<div class="note"><strong>A class on a group matches the group&#39;s contents too.</strong> <code>$S(&#39;.piece&#39;)</code> returns the four groups <em>and</em> their tiles, and each tile&#39;s black border besides, so the count at the end of the block filters by name. This is the same thing the Pythagorean short&#39;s note about <code>.cube</code> says; here it matters whenever a verb is applied to a class: <code>.fade</code> on <code>.piece</code> would fade the tiles one by one, which is what you want for a glass effect but not for a move.</div>

<h2><span class="step">4</span> The outline, the rulers and the cuts</h2>

<p class="lede">Four thin white bars mark the outline of the whole square; it is what the pieces come from and go back to, and it stays behind, faint, while they are apart. Along the top and down the left side, two rulers show how each side splits: <em>p</em> in coral, the colour of <em>A</em>, and <em>q</em> in aqua, the colour of <em>a</em>; they use the coral and aqua materials of the tiles. The two cuts are thin white bars that are a little proud of the tiles, so they read as lines drawn across the square. Rulers and cuts start at scale 0.001 on every axis (invisible) and <em>grow</em> when their moment comes: because they share a material with other objects, they cannot be faded one at a time.</p>

<h2><span class="step">5</span> Five cameras</h2>

<p class="lede">A shot camera is an ordinary camera with a track of keys: <code>setKeys([{ t, pos, lookAt }, …], { smooth: true })</code> plays one smooth curve through them (see <a href="https://strataeditor.dev/guides/CAMERA_PATHS.md">Editing camera moves</a>). <code>orbit</code> writes the keys from an azimuth, a radius and a height. Because this scene is flat, the cameras stay nearly head-on (azimuth within ±8°): a square seen from the side is a sliver, and the equation above it, a row of text that faces the front, would shrink. Each is aimed a little above the middle of what it films. The second one is further back and aimed lower, because during the sort the pieces fly up towards the equation. The third is closer and aimed to the right of the middle, for the allele count, so that the list of assumptions has room beside the bins; the fourth pulls back for the finished square, and the fifth faces the end card.</p>

<h2><span class="step">6</span> The labels</h2>

<p class="lede">Open <strong>Stencils → Mesh → Text</strong> and click it <em>once</em>; the next block copies that one label for every other, so they all share its font, and gives each the shared material of its colour (white, coral, gold or aqua) instead of a copy of its own. A label&#39;s text starts at the label&#39;s own origin, so to centre a label you subtract half its width. The widths come from the font: the sum of its glyphs&#39; advances, in units of the font size, times the label&#39;s scale.</p>

At a scale of 0.6 and a gap of 0.45 between nine labels the equation is 12.1 wide, so its first label starts at −6.05, and each next one at the sum of the widths and gaps before it. The letters on the pieces are _children_ of their piece (`parent.add(label)`), so they travel, shrink and turn with it.

<div class="note"><strong>Shared materials: no cross-fade, and one selector per object.</strong> The first version of this scene gave every label, ruler and cut a material of its own, 34 in all, so that each could fade by itself. A material belongs to every mesh that holds it, so a fade on one is a fade on all. The film now has nine: three for the room, coral, gold and aqua (tiles, rulers, tokens and coloured labels), white (white labels, cuts, bins), the outline, which fades as a whole, and the end card. The price is that labels appear with a cut (<code>change(text)</code> with no <code>transition</code>) and rulers and cuts <em>grow</em> instead of fading. The other rule is that every event for one object goes through <em>one</em> selector: <code>.rulertop</code> and <code>#RulerTopP</code> would both write the same scale track, and the ruler would drift to the wrong size.</div>

<div class="note"><strong>Ids are matched without regard to case.</strong> A label called <code>kAA</code> and another called <code>kaa</code> are the same id to the selector engine, so one of them is silently left out of any set that names both. The first version of this scene did exactly that, and it showed <em>aa</em> on the coral piece. Name labels for <em>where</em> they are (<code>kidPP</code>, <code>kidQQ</code>), never for what they say.</div>

<h2><span class="step">12</span> The allele count</h2>

<p class="lede">This is the beat that turns the square into the law. The square has shown how often each kind of child occurs; it has not shown that the alleles <em>A</em> and <em>a</em> are as common in the children as in the parents. To show it, count them. There are 25 children (the 25 tiles), and each carries two alleles, so each tile hands out two little cubes: <em>AA</em> two coral, <em>aa</em> two aqua, <em>Aa</em> one of each. The cubes are coloured by <strong>allele</strong>, not by genotype: a gold tile hands out one coral and one aqua cube. They stream into two bins, and the arithmetic is in the code&#39;s comment: 18 + 12 = <strong>30 <em>A</em></strong> and 12 + 8 = <strong>20 <em>a</em></strong>.</p>

The count is a _measurement_, not a line of arithmetic. A cube is 0.42 wide on a pitch of 0.5, so a row of six is 2.92 long and a row of four is 1.92, the exact lengths of the two rulers that measured the parents (3 − 0.08 and 2 − 0.08). <h2><span class="step">16</span> Name and class everything</h2>

<p class="lede">This scene was first published before every object had to be named and classed, so this last block was added afterwards; paste it after all the others. The film is only <em>addressable</em> if every object has a name an author chose and at least one class: otherwise <code>$S(&#39;#id&#39;)</code>, <code>$S(&#39;.class&#39;)</code>, an AI edit or a tutorial step cannot reach it. The block names the few objects the build left anonymous or shared (from where they sit), gives every object its classes from one table, and calls <code>requireLabels(true)</code>: from then on a commit is <em>refused</em> while any object is unnamed, duplicated or without a class, and <code>lint()</code> lists what is wrong. The four frame bars are now <code>FrameEdgeBottom</code>, <code>FrameEdgeTop</code>, <code>FrameEdgeLeft</code> and <code>FrameEdgeRight</code>, and each bin&#39;s three boxes <code>binBigA_floor</code>, <code>_left</code>, <code>_right</code> (and the same for <code>binLittleA</code>); <code>$S(&#39;.frameedge&#39;)</code> still selects the bars because the table gives each of them that class.</p>
