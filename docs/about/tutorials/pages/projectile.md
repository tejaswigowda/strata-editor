# A projectile: two motions, one path

<div class="wc-embed" title="A projectile: two motions, one path: interactive example"><a href="https://strataeditor.dev/#repo=tejaswigowda/test1@3bd6749e4d9d28a292160ce9d5d543df2447dc75&amp;file=projectile.json&amp;present=true">Open embed</a></div>

<p class="tagline">A 79-second visual argument that the curve a thrown ball makes is two simple motions at once: sideways at a constant speed, and up and down under gravity. Each motion is shown alone (a shadow on the ground, a shadow on a pole), the camera orbits so you can look at either one by itself, and then they are put back together. The same two motions, with a different angle or speed, give a different curve. Built with the JS Shell, with exact physics.</p>

<h2><span class="step">1</span> The idea</h2>

<p class="lede">Launch a ball with speed <em>v</em> at an angle <em>θ</em>. It has a sideways speed <em>v<sub>x</sub> = v cos θ</em> and an upward speed <em>v<sub>y</sub> = v sin θ</em>. Nothing pushes it sideways, so <em>v<sub>x</sub></em> never changes: <em>x = v<sub>x</sub> t</em>. Gravity pulls it down, so <em>v<sub>y</sub></em> changes by the same amount every second: <em>y = v<sub>y</sub> t − ½ g t²</em>. Neither formula mentions the other. That is the key lesson: <strong>independent components combine into one path</strong>.</p>

**The 3D advantage.** From the side the two motions are tangled. Orbit the path and they come apart: seen from above, along the ground, the ball moves at an even pace and the up-and-down motion vanishes; seen from the pole's side, only the height shows. The film uses two _shadows_ to say the same thing in a picture: an aqua ball that slides along the ground under the real one, and a coral ball that rides a pole beside it.

The units are scene units in slow motion (so that a flight lasts a few seconds): _g = 2.5_, ball radius _0.25_, the ball launched from, and landing on, the ground, with a base speed of _6.32_. The numbers the film uses, all computed from the two formulas:

<h2><span class="step">2</span> The palette, a room and a floor</h2>

<p class="lede">The ten materials of the film are made once, here, and every mesh that uses a colour is handed the same one: aqua is the sideways motion, coral is the up and down, gold is the path, purple is gravity, silver is the ball. A shared material cannot fade one mesh without fading all of them, so this film reveals things by <em>growing</em> them from a scale of 0.001 and removes them by shrinking. The floor has a grid of lines every unit and a heavier one every five, so the equal steps can be counted.</p>

<h2><span class="step">4</span> The physics, written as two tracks</h2>

<p class="lede">The film&#39;s physics is not simulated, it is <em>drawn from the two formulas</em>, each as its own animation. The sideways motion is a linear track. The up and down motion is two eased tracks: the rise slows evenly, which is a quadratic ease-out, and the fall speeds up evenly, which is a quadratic ease-in. Both are exactly parabolas, and both are written as the cubic Bézier that has that shape, <code>cubic-bezier(0.3333,0.6667,0.6667,1)</code> for the rise and <code>cubic-bezier(0.3333,0,0.6667,0.3333)</code> for the fall. A ball is two nested groups (the outer one slides along x, the inner one goes up and down), so each track belongs to a different object and nothing fights.</p>

Every animation is queued with its time (`ev`) and written in time order at the very end (`flush`), so no object's track is ever written out of order. The same block makes the arrows that ride on the ball: the aqua one never changes, the coral up arrow shortens evenly to nothing at the top and the down arrow lengthens evenly from nothing, the purple one (gravity) never changes. It also builds the trail, a gold dot every tenth of a second along the exact path, each popping in as the ball reaches it.

<h2><span class="step">13</span> Name and class everything</h2>

<p class="lede">The film is only <em>addressable</em> if every object has a name an author chose and at least one class: otherwise <code>$S(&#39;#b0&#39;)</code>, <code>$S(&#39;.trail-dot&#39;)</code>, an AI edit or a tutorial step cannot reach it. Every object was named where it was created (a grid line is <code>gridX07</code>, a tick on the pole <code>poleTick3</code>, the ball inside <code>b0</code> is <code>b0_sphere</code>: one template string in the loop that makes them), so this last block only adds the classes, from one table, and then calls <code>requireLabels(true)</code>. That marks the scene: from now on a commit is <em>refused</em> while any object is unnamed, duplicated or without a class, and <code>lint()</code> lists what is wrong. Objects the engine makes itself (the dark shadow of each text label, whose names start with <code>__</code>) are exempt.</p>

<pre><code>// Every object has a name (set where it is created) and now a class. Classes are what make the scene queryable: $S(&#39;.arrow&#39;), $S(&#39;.trail-dot&#39;), $S(&#39;.sideways&#39;).&#10;// One table, first column the names it covers, second the classes it gives; an object can match several rows. Anything left without a class is listed and the block stops.&#10;const rules = [&#10;  [/^(Room|Floor)$/, [&#39;environment&#39;]], [/^(Grid|grid[XZ]\d+)$/, [&#39;environment&#39;, &#39;grid&#39;]], [/Light$/, [&#39;light&#39;]],&#10;  [/^(CannonBase|barrel\d+|barrelTube\d+)$/, [&#39;cannon&#39;]], [/^barrel\d+$/, [&#39;barrel&#39;]],&#10;  [/^(pole|poleRod|poleTick\d+)$/, [&#39;pole&#39;]], [/^(groundLine|groundBar|groundTick\d+)$/, [&#39;ground-line&#39;]],&#10;  [/^b[012](y|_sphere)?$/, [&#39;ball&#39;]],&#10;  [/^(arr[XVGUD]|gx_|gy_|gr_)/, [&#39;arrow&#39;]], [/^(arrX|gx_)/, [&#39;sideways&#39;]], [/^(arrV|arrU|arrD|gy_)/, [&#39;vertical&#39;]], [/^arrG/, [&#39;gravity&#39;]], [/^gr_/, [&#39;resultant&#39;]],&#10;  [/^sh[XY]/, [&#39;shadow&#39;]], [/^shX/, [&#39;sideways&#39;]], [/^shY/, [&#39;vertical&#39;]],&#10;  [/^trail\w+$/, [&#39;trail&#39;]], [/^trail\w+_\d+$/, [&#39;trail-dot&#39;]],&#10;  [/^ring/, [&#39;landing-ring&#39;]], [/^(gh_|ga_)/, [&#39;ghost&#39;]], [/^t[xy]_\d+$/, [&#39;bead&#39;]],&#10;  [/^(lnd|acr|ud|eq|grav|zero|a30|a45|a60|same|slow|fast)$/, [&#39;label&#39;]],&#10;  [/^cam/, [&#39;camera&#39;]], [/^callcard$/, [&#39;card&#39;]]&#10;];&#10;const left = [];&#10;scene.traverse(o =&gt; {&#10;  if (o === scene || o.name.startsWith(&#39;__&#39;)) return;&#10;  const add = rules.filter(([re]) =&gt; re.test(o.name)).flatMap(([, c]) =&gt; c);&#10;  if (add.length === 0) { left.push(o.name || &#39;(unnamed)&#39;); return; }&#10;  o.userData.customClasses = [...new Set([...(o.userData.customClasses || []), ...add])];&#10;});&#10;if (left.length) throw new Error(left.length + &#39; objects have no class: &#39; + left.slice(0, 12).join(&#39;, &#39;));&#10;requireLabels(true)      // from here on a commit is refused while anything is unnamed, duplicated or classless</code></pre>

<p class="lede">The scene is <a href="https://strataeditor.dev/#repo=tejaswigowda/test1&amp;file=projectile.json">projectile.json</a> in <code>tejaswigowda/test1</code>. <div class="note"><strong>Why the player at the top is pinned to a commit.</strong> The player reads the scene through a CDN that can keep serving a branch&#39;s previous version for hours after a push, so a link by branch name could show the version before this page was written. The player&#39;s address names the commit (<code>@3bd6749</code>), which never goes stale; the link in this step reads the branch head. When the scene is republished, move the commit in the player&#39;s address.</div>

<div class="note"><strong>What was checked, and what differs.</strong> Every block on this page was pasted, one paste at a time, in this order, into an empty scene, with the Text stencil and the Call card stencil clicked where the text says so; the scene was then committed and loaded again from that commit, and read back: ten materials of its own (each text label also makes a dark shadow material when it is shown), 46 geometries, 434 objects (every one named and classed: <code>lint()</code> reports nothing, and the scene is marked with <code>requireLabels</code>), 251 timeline tracks, 8 shots and 12 cues. The ball&#39;s position was read back at 35 moments across five flights (30°, 45° and 60° at speed 1, and 45° at speeds 0.75 and 1.25) and compared with <em>x = v<sub>x</sub> t</em> and <em>y = 0.25 + v<sub>y</sub> t − ½ g t²</em>: the largest difference was 0.002 scene units. The screenshots are frames of the pasted scene at 2.6, 9, 14, 24, 30.5, 38, 49.5, 55, 58.5, 67 and 78 s, with the editor&#39;s controls hidden. Rendering to a video file (step 15) was not run for this page. The film shows exact physics for an ideal projectile, with no air resistance, in slow motion; the ghost arrows are drawn at a smaller scale than the arrows on the ball, so they do not overlap.</div>
