Cut a scene into named parts

An avocado has three texture islands and each one is a part. A furnished room is one mesh with 538 islands, most of them a few triangles of trim or the label on a prop, and no island is "the bed". SEG handles the part of the job that needs no judgment: it joins the islands that touch into components, measures them in the world frame, places everything you did not name on the nearest thing you did, and checks the result. What is left for you, or for a model, is the one step that never reduced to a rule: naming the anchors. Built with the JS Shell.

Start with the object tutorial. This page assumes Cut any textured mesh into named parts (the avocado and the duck): SEG.describe, SEG.split, SEG.preview, the plan, and the record. The reference is guides/MESH_SEGMENT.md. An older page, building the tool by hand, is for the object case and uses the name CUT.

1 Same tool, different evidence

The object tutorial's evidence was mostly colour: the cut face of the avocado is cream and orange, the beak of the duck is orange. A room has almost none to give. Its texture is a dark green-black atlas shared by a few hundred pieces, and the two lines that matter most for telling a ceiling from a floor are not colours at all: they are where a piece is and how big. The tool is the same; the evidence that carries the weight is different, and the report changes with it.

An object (avocado, duck)A scene (the room)
Grainthe islandthe component: islands that touch in space
The report leads withtexture position, then colourssize and position in the world, colours last
What tells parts apartpaint: rings, eyes, a beakposition, size, how much detail: a slab at the bottom is a floor, a 1,892-triangle figure 0.7 high is a cat
Who places the small piecesyou, island by islandthe host, by distance, from the anchors you name

Who chooses the grain. The host does, from the mesh: when there are at most three components for every four islands (the room has 538 islands and 282 components), SEG.describe and SEG.data report components; otherwise islands. A caller can say which: SEG.describe('#Mesh', 3, { grain: 'islands' }). The choice is stated in the last line of the report, and the grain the plan was written against is recorded.

282 lines instead of 538, and each one says what a person asks first: how big, where, how detailed. Component 21 is 118 triangles, 5.7 × 0.47 × 7.7, with at.y 0.96: a thin slab at the very top, a ceiling. Component 29 is 75 triangles, 9.2 × 1.0 × 7.0, at.y 0.09: a slab at the bottom, the floor deck. Component 1 is 1,892 triangles, 0.29 × 0.70 × 1.16, low and in the middle: far too detailed and too small to be furniture. Its colours are grey and white. A cat.

Why "world frame" is a line of its own. The model is Z-up, and the importer turns it upright with a rotation. In the mesh's frame the ceiling has at.z 0.04 and the floor 0.91: "up" is on the wrong axis and runs the wrong way, and every number looks fine. SEG.components and the scene report apply the node's world matrix, so y is up and 0.96 is high. SEG.describe on an object still reports in the mesh's frame, as its tutorial says; a record says which it used (attach.parameters.frame).

The same numbers as objects, for a script or a model: SEG.components('#Object_4'). Each has component, islands, triangles, size, centre, at and colours.

4 Name the anchors

This is the step that does not reduce to a rule, and the only one that is yours. An anchor is a component you can name from the evidence. Start with the biggest and most distinctive; the rest will follow them. Say what each name rests on: position, size, colour, or shape when all you can honestly offer is a description of the form. It is recorded, and a record with many shape names is a different claim from one with none.

Before cutting, ask where everything else would go:

SEG.attach('#Object_4', [
  { name: 'Floor_Deck',    classes: ['floor'],   component: 29 },
  { name: 'Cyber_Cat',     classes: ['cat'],     component: 1 },
  { name: 'Ceiling_Panel', classes: ['ceiling'], component: 21 } ], { maxDistance: 0.5 })

It returns parts (each anchor with what would be attached), unattached (what is too far), tie_broken, the parameters used and, per part, how many components it would take and how far the furthest was. Nothing is cut.

5 What attaching does, exactly

The rules are fixed and written down, so a run can be repeated and a result can be questioned.

  1. Distance is to triangles, not vertices. A floor is a few huge triangles, and its four corner vertices are metres from anything standing on it. Each unnamed component is sampled at the centres of up to six triangles per island, and the distance is the shortest from a sample to any triangle of the anchor.
  2. The nearest anchor wins, but near-ties go to the smaller thing. Every anchor within tie (0.05 world units) of the nearest is tied with it, and the one with the smaller bounding box wins, so a mug that touches both the counter and the floor belongs to the counter. A prop beats the structure it stands against. On the room the rule decided seven components, each given to a smaller anchor than the strictly nearest one. tie is a recorded parameter, in world units: change it if your scene is a hundred times bigger.
  3. Beyond maxDistance, nothing is attached. There is always a nearest anchor, so without a limit a lamp standing alone across the room would join the nearest wall, and a component that is really an object of its own would be absorbed without a word. The limit defaults to a tenth of the scene's diagonal (1.448 on this room, which is 14.48 across) and the value used is recorded. A component beyond it is returned to you, named with its nearest anchor and the distance, and the cut refuses to run until you name it as an anchor of its own, or describe it the way Dark_Console_Unit is described, or raise the limit.

7 The checks, and the cut they caught

The first cut of this room was wrong in a way the picture did not show. The thin brown trim that runs round the whole room was attached to the nearest anchor, which was Wall_Front_Right_Column, and the column came out room-sized. What gave it away was a number, not the picture: the part's box spanned 70%, 92% and 99% of the room on x, y and z while it carried 14% of the surface. That is now an assertion, and it fires on exactly that plan.

AssertionFires when
oversizea part's box spans at least 60% of the scene on every axis while it carries less than 25% of the surface: trim, or several objects, swallowed
engulfinga part's box contains at least half of the other parts' centres while it carries less than 10% of the surface
absorbeda part assembled from at least 8 components whose main colour holds at least 85% of its texture footprint
orphan sinkone part received at least half of the components that were attached, out of at least 10

A part that declares itself spread out (a class of trim, frame, cable, pipe, wire, rail, network, edge or outline) is exempt from oversize and engulfing, which is how the fix is expressed: name the room-wide trim as one part and say so, as in step 6. Fewer than four parts are not judged. All the thresholds are in ASSERTIONS in meshSegment.js and are recorded with every cut.

A fired check does not stop the cut and does not change it. The cut is one undo step, and the right response to a failed check, undo and cut again with a better plan, is a decision for whoever wrote the plan. The check says which assertion, which part, and the numbers.

The one still firing is real. On the corrected cut, orphan sink still says Plant_Cluster took 167 of the 259 attached components (64%). It did: its 168 components average about seven triangles each, they lie along the right edge, and no anchor claims them, so the plant is the nearest. What they are (leaves, curtain folds, clutter) was not checked. The cut is usable, but "Plant_Cluster" is the room's catch-all, and the warning is the tool saying so. The remedy is the one in the message: name more anchors for what is there, and cut again. The tutorial leaves the warning in place on purpose.

8 What the record keeps

const rec = $S('#Cyber_Room_Parts').nodes[0].userData.segmentation
rec.grain                              // 'components'
rec.plan                               // the anchors, names, classes, basis, source: as given
rec.describe.components                // the 282-line report the decision was made from
rec.attach.parameters                  // { max_distance: 1.448, max_distance_default: true, tie: 0.05, samples_per_island: 6, weld_digits: 4, scene_diagonal: 14.48, frame: 'world' }
rec.attach.per_part                    // per part: components, how many were attached, and the median, 90th percentile and maximum distance
rec.attach.tie_broken                  // the seven components the tie-break gave to a smaller anchor than the nearest
rec.attach.unattached                  // [] : nothing was left over
rec.signals.components_per_part        // Plant_Cluster 168, Crate_Stack 24, Cyber_Cat 1, …: a part made of 168 components is a different claim from one made of 1
rec.signals.names_by_basis             // { evidence: 13, shape: 1, unstated: 0 }
rec.signals.assertions                 // what fired, with the numbers; rec.signals.assertion_parameters says what the thresholds were

SEG.replay('#Object_4', rec) recomputes the components and the attachment from the recorded plan and parameters, and refuses to run if the mesh is not the one the record describes (different triangle counts, or different islands in a component). Run on a fresh import, it gave the same 14 parts, triangle for triangle, and the same attachment record.

9 Your own scene

  1. SEG.describe('#mesh'). If it reports islands, the mesh is an object: use the object tutorial. If it reports components, read size, centre and at first.
  2. Name the 8 to 20 components that you can justify, biggest first. Give each a basis. Use shape for what you can only describe.
  3. SEG.attach('#mesh', anchors) to see where everything goes and what is left over. Name what is left, or raise maxDistance if you mean it.
  4. SEG.split. Read every ⚠. Declare trim as trim. If a part took most of what was attached, name more anchors.
  5. SEG.preview and isolate from every side; then lint() and requireLabels(true).
What was checked, and what was not. The functions behind this page are tested in node on a synthetic room of boxes (components, the world frame, the tie-break, the limit, each assertion and a recorded plan run again) and on the real room, from the .glb file alone with no browser and no model: 538 islands become 282 components; the 14 anchors of the first cut give the same triangles and islands per part as that cut did when it was made by hand; the first plan fires oversize on Wall_Front_Right_Column and orphan sink on Plant_Cluster; a limit of 0.3 leaves exactly five components unattached. In the editor served from this repository, on a fresh import of the model, these were run: the report, the SEG.attach call of step 4, the cut of step 6 with its record, an undo, and the replay. The refusal at maxDistance: 0.3 was checked in node, not in the browser. SEG.preview and isolate were not run on this cut: look at it yourself. Not checked: any model's ability to choose the anchors (that step was done by hand, and its names are my reading of the evidence, not a measurement); the thresholds, which were set on one room and one failure; and Object_5 and Object_6, which were left whole.
What a record cannot do. It cannot prove that the licence or attribution you give is right (SEG only refuses to run without them); it cannot say that the names are correct, only what they were made from, and now also by what rule everything else was placed.
